# The Form Asked My Permission to Share My Health Data. Then It Wouldn't Let Me Say No.

**Signal Noise | Privacy & Surveillance | Sable Ren | 2026-06-05**

## Summary

Healthcare registration platforms used by tens of millions of Americans describe a patient's right to opt out of data sharing — then offer no mechanism to exercise it. A joint investigation by The Markup and CalMatters, published May 27, 2026, names three vendors and documents the gap between what the notice says and what the form allows. The Director of the Department of Health and Human Services' Office for Civil Rights is among those who encountered the problem firsthand.

## Body

Phreesia's patient intake software touches what the company says is one in six patient visits in the United States. Somewhere in that flow, patients encounter a privacy notice. The notice describes their right to opt out of health information exchange. Below the description is a button.

The button says "I accept." There is no other option.

The Markup and CalMatters documented this pattern in a joint investigation published May 27, 2026, by Alex Rosenblat, director of sociotechnical research at The Markup. Rosenblat's team tested the intake flow firsthand at a Virginia women's health clinic — including an October 2025 telehealth session — and conducted interviews with more than 20 patients, healthcare providers, experts, and advocates. Phreesia is not the only vendor named. Athenahealth and Privia Health — which, according to a 2025 Privia press release, manages services for nearly 5,000 providers across 15 states and a patient population of 5.2 million — were also identified in the investigation.

Paula Stannard, Director of the Office for Civil Rights at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, described her own experience with one of these forms at the HIMSS 2026 conference in March. "Had I received the notice of privacy practices? No," she told the audience. She added that she had written a note on the form: "I have not received this. I am not acknowledging receipt."

Her account exposes a specific legal gap. HIPAA's Notice of Privacy Practices provision, codified at 45 CFR §164.520, does not require a patient's signature. It requires only acknowledgment of receipt — and permits providers to document the reason no acknowledgment was obtained. The regulation was not designed for a form that confirms receipt without allowing refusal.

State law creates an uneven floor above the federal baseline. Florida and New York require explicit opt-in consent for certain data-sharing arrangements. Arizona and Maryland operate on opt-out defaults. The Markup's investigation documented the single-button intake pattern across multiple states.

The federal regulatory picture has been in motion for years. HHS OCR proposed a rule in 2021 that would eliminate the written acknowledgment requirement entirely. It has not been finalized. If it were, it would remove the last procedural record showing that patients had been told they had a choice.
