## Summary

Lime's largest emissions category—the manufacturing of its scooters and e-bikes—dropped 50% year-over-year while the company was preparing its Nasdaq debut. The mechanism is purchasing leverage rather than operational control: Lime presses contract suppliers toward renewable-electricity aluminum and lower-carbon batteries, extended vehicle lifespans, and biofuel logistics. How far that leverage scales beyond a 230-city fleet is the question the figures don't answer.

## Body

The constraint that defines Lime's emissions story is structural: the company produces nothing. Every scooter and e-bike that reaches a Lime rack comes from contract manufacturers in which Lime holds no equity and no direct authority over energy sourcing. When the company reported roughly 90,000 metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent in company-associated emissions for 2025—a 35% absolute reduction from its 2019 baseline despite ridership kilometers growing by nearly 250% over the same period—it was describing what purchasing leverage and design choices can accomplish, not operational control. Those are meaningful instruments, but different ones.

The 50% year-over-year reduction in capital-goods emissions between 2024 and 2025 is the most concrete number in Lime's 2025 sustainability reporting, as covered by Heatmap News. Capital goods is Scope 3, Category 2 under the Greenhouse Gas Protocol: emissions from producing the vehicles themselves. Lime achieved the reduction primarily by pushing manufacturers to use aluminum smelted with renewable electricity and batteries with lower-carbon production profiles. Andrew Savage, Lime's vice president of sustainability, described the mechanism to Heatmap as significant influence without ownership: "We don't own the manufacturing, but we have a large amount of input."

The cost of that input, Lime says, was "marginal." The claim invites scrutiny. The premium for renewable-energy aluminum varies considerably by market and by the smelter's existing energy mix—a facility already running on hydropower may carry no cost premium at all; one on a coal-heavy grid represents a genuine incremental cost. The brief does not supply Lime's actual procurement cost delta, so "marginal" stands as a company characterization, not an independently verified figure.

On logistics, Lime made two moves worth distinguishing. At the Los Angeles and Long Beach ports—the primary North American entry points for hardware shipped from Asian factories—it shifted to electric drayage trucks in 2024. In Europe, it adopted HVO100 hydrotreated vegetable oil as a diesel substitute for hub-to-city transport across 16 cities. HVO100 carries a lifecycle emissions figure of roughly 89% below conventional diesel on a well-to-wheel basis—when it is produced from waste feedstocks.

That qualifier carries weight. Nikita Pavlenko, a researcher at the International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT), told Heatmap that HVO100 supply is constrained and that the biofuel's emissions profile is not uniform: purpose-grown crops rather than waste vegetable oil or animal-fat feedstocks introduce land-use change emissions that can substantially erode the lifecycle advantage, with deforestation risk compounding the calculation. Lime's own sustainability vice president acknowledged HVO100 as an interim solution, citing the limited commercial availability of electrified heavy trucking even in Norway, a market where EV passenger penetration is among the world's highest.

One additional logistics note: despite the overall category reduction, Lime reported that upstream shipping emissions increased slightly in 2025 due to expedited parts shipping. Supply chain decarbonization is not linear across all subcategories simultaneously.

Two headline figures in Lime's reporting require careful attribution. The 38 million car trips replaced in 2025 is Lime's own self-reported estimate for calendar year 2025, based on Lime's substitution methodology. Independent measurement of car-trip displacement in shared micromobility is methodologically contested: rider-survey results depend heavily on how the counterfactual trip is defined and whether induced demand is accounted for. Lime's own sustainability website reports more than 120 million car trips replaced cumulatively and a finding that a Lime trip uses 75% less carbon than a comparable car trip; those figures and the annual 38 million are not directly comparable, as they represent different scopes and time frames. The ~90,000 tCO₂e figure is similarly self-reported for 2025, and the brief does not specify which Scope 3 categories are included in or excluded from that total. Lime's 2030 targets—a 90% absolute reduction in Scope 1 and 2 by 2030, and a 97% reduction in Scope 3 per rider-kilometer intensity by 2030—use different baseline units for Scope 3 (intensity, not absolute), a distinction that matters if ridership scales faster than per-kilometer emissions fall.

The company's overall emissions trajectory since 2019 is worth noting: 81% reduction in carbon intensity per kilometer traveled, and 35% absolute reduction in total emissions, against ridership growth of nearly 250%. That combination describes real progress in decoupling emissions from use. It does not describe zero emissions, and it does not independently verify the mechanisms by which the decarbonization occurred.

Lime raised approximately $174 million through its July 1, 2026 Nasdaq IPO, priced at $25 per share under the ticker LIME. That figure includes proceeds to selling shareholders; new-share proceeds to Lime were approximately $167 million, as corroborated by wire reporting. The company now operates across 230 cities on five continents.
