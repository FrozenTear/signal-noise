## Summary

Massachusetts is running a two-year Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) demonstration that pays a pre-selected group of EV owners to discharge battery power to the grid during summer peak demand, working through the ConnectedSolutions virtual-power-plant platform operated by the state's investor-owned utilities. The participating cohort is closed: roughly 30 to 45 residential customers, five school districts, and four municipal projects — approximately 60 to 80 bidirectional chargers installed at no cost. A January 2026 Massachusetts utility ruling prohibits participants from stacking V2G payments with net-metering compensation on the same meter, a constraint the demonstration's design does not resolve.

---

## Body

The binding constraint in Massachusetts' first vehicle-to-grid demonstration is not whether bidirectional charging technology can work — it can, and the state's investor-owned utilities already have a payment mechanism in place through their ConnectedSolutions virtual-power-plant program. The constraint is whether the economics work at the point that matters: the vehicle purchase decision.

The Massachusetts Clean Energy Center (MassCEC) and the Healey-Driscoll Administration announced the demonstration's participant cohort in February 2026. The operational phase — charger installation, grid-discharge data collection — is underway as of late July 2026 and expected to run through the end of summer 2026. Two summers of data from a closed, pre-selected group is the program's scope. Massachusetts EV owners who were not selected cannot join it.

---

**Who is in the cohort**

Five school districts, four municipal projects, and approximately 30 to 45 individual residential customers make up the participant pool. Roughly 60 to 80 bidirectional Level 2 charging systems will be installed at no cost to participants. The $50 million in program funding came from 2021 American Rescue Plan Act appropriations — federal pandemic-relief dollars redirected into grid infrastructure, per the MassCEC press release and Utility Dive.

Vehicles eligible for the residential and light-duty cohort include the Ford F-150 Lightning, Nissan Leaf, Kia EV9, Polestar 3, and the Volvo EX90 — the all-battery-electric model, confirmed by Electrek and Utility Dive (distinct from the plug-in hybrid XC90). The Tesla Cybertruck is not eligible, per Heatmap, because Tesla places the requisite DC-to-AC power converter within the vehicle itself — whereas every other eligible model places it within the wall charger — an architecture that does not align with current utility certification processes.

Heatmap also names Sunrun, The Mobility House, and EnergyHub as technology partners for the demonstration. Those attributions appear in Heatmap's coverage but are not confirmed in the MassCEC press release available to this reporter; they should be treated as single-sourced to Heatmap until corroborated against program documentation.

---

**What participants earn**

ConnectedSolutions pays enrolled resources for demand reduction during summer afternoon peak hours — typically weekday afternoons from June through September. The base rate is approximately $275 per average kilowatt of summer grid support, per Heatmap News. For light-duty passenger vehicles, Utility Dive estimates annual earnings of roughly $3,000 per summer.

School buses carry significantly larger battery packs and higher earning potential. Utility Dive cites estimates of up to $12,000 per year via National Grid's ConnectedSolutions tariff.

The Beverly, Massachusetts, public school district offers the closest historical benchmark: its electric bus discharged 10.78 megawatt-hours across summers 2021 and 2022 in an earlier MassCEC demonstration, generating approximately $23,500 in ConnectedSolutions payments, per Utility Dive. The current demonstration draws on that earlier data to extend V2G payments into the residential-vehicle scale.

---

**The net-metering stacking problem**

As of January 2026, Massachusetts investor-owned utility rules do not permit a customer to collect net-metering compensation and V2G discharge payments simultaneously when a bidirectional charger shares a utility meter with an existing net-metering-eligible solar installation. The two compensation streams cannot be stacked on the same meter.

Customers with rooftop solar who are considering V2G enrollment should verify their metering configuration with their utility before assuming both programs pay out concurrently. The constraint is not unique to Massachusetts — it reflects a structural gap between the net-energy-metering framework (designed for solar generation) and the virtual-power-plant tariff framework (designed for demand flexibility). Closing that gap requires action at the Department of Public Utilities level and in the utilities' interconnection and tariff proceedings. The demonstration itself does not address it.

---

**Scope and what the data will resolve**

Multiple outlets covered the demonstration across a seven-week window: Utility Dive published June 2, 2026; Heatmap News published July 22; Electrek and the Boston Globe both published July 23; Canary Media published in July 2026. The Healey-Driscoll Administration framed the July operational-phase coverage as a public announcement. This was not an exclusive — the story had been in trade press since June. The Boston Globe's conditional register — vehicle-to-grid "could pay thousands" to EV owners — is the accurate one: conditional, contingent on selection into a closed program, and tied to a summer-peak earning window.

The MassCEC participant-selection announcement is dated February 2, 2026; the July 2026 coverage coincides with the operational phase beginning, not a new program launch.

Two summers of data from 60 to 80 bidirectional chargers can plausibly answer whether ConnectedSolutions rates produce sufficient grid-side demand reduction to justify a larger program design. What the data cannot answer is whether the payment rate alone — absent a charger-cost subsidy or a change in vehicle purchase incentives — would shift buyer behavior toward bidirectional-capable models in the broader Massachusetts market. That requires a different study design, and probably a different policy instrument: a bidirectional-charger mandate, a direct equipment subsidy at point of sale, or an interconnect-queue or tariff reform that makes the economics visible at the vehicle dealership level rather than after years of grid dispatch.

MassCEC has framed this as a groundbreaking demonstration. The Beverly school bus data from 2021–2022 suggests the bus-scale economics are demonstrable. Whether residential-scale economics follow — where the buyer must first choose a bidirectional-capable vehicle, possibly at a price premium — is what the next two summers are designed to test.
