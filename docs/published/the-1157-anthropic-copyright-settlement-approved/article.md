# Judge signs off on Anthropic's $1.5 billion copyright settlement with authors

Federal Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín granted final approval Monday to a $1.5 billion settlement between Anthropic and a class of authors who claimed the AI company ingested their books without authorization to train its Claude models. Bloomberg Law, Reuters, and TechCrunch all reported the approval; Bloomberg Law and Reuters describe it as the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history.

The case, *Bartz v. Anthropic*, was brought by authors Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson, who served as class representatives for a broader group of affected authors. Reuters, the Authors Guild, and TechCrunch report the settlement fund will distribute approximately $3,000 per eligible work across an estimated 500,000 works. Both the per-work rate and the work count are estimates; the final distribution will depend on the claims process.

## Two judges, two rulings

Judge Martínez-Olguín's approval should not be conflated with the underlying substantive ruling that shaped the litigation. That ruling came from Judge William Alsup, who has since retired. In a summary judgment, Alsup held that training large language models on books constitutes fair use, and separately ruled that Anthropic's use of the LibGen and PiLiMi piracy databases did not. The two holdings address different conduct: one concerns how Claude was trained; the other concerns where the training data originated.

The settlement closes the case. It does not generate circuit-court precedent, because a settled case produces no appellate ruling. Alsup's fair use holding stands as a district-court opinion but has not been reviewed or affirmed at the Ninth Circuit level.

## What the settlement resolves — and what it does not

Anthropic paid a significant sum to close a case in which a district court had already ruled in its favor on the core training question. The $1.5 billion figure represents a negotiated resolution of both the piracy-track claims (LibGen, PiLiMi) and the broader class's copyright claims — approved by the court, not a damages verdict following a liability finding.

Reading the settlement as a concession that AI training on copyrighted books is infringement goes beyond what the settlement says.

Kluwer Copyright Blog and TechCrunch both note that the agreement binds only the parties in this case. The fair use question remains live in parallel proceedings against Google, Meta, Midjourney, and OpenAI, where courts have not issued comparable holdings and litigation is at varying stages. The Authors Guild, which advocated on behalf of affected members throughout the case, has published a summary of settlement terms for authors weighing their options under the claims process.

## The broader AI copyright landscape

*Bartz v. Anthropic* is the first major financial resolution in the wave of copyright suits that AI developers have faced since large language models became commercially significant. The $1.5 billion number will circulate as a reference point in the broader AI-and-copyright debate. What it cannot supply is a definitive answer to the underlying legal question: the settlement structure ensures that the most consequential facts in the case were never tested to a verdict.

That question — whether training on copyrighted works is permissible under U.S. copyright law — will be answered, if at all, by the cases still in active litigation.
